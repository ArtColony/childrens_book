# Manuscript review: picture-book editor

*`reviewer-writer` on Opus. Given the ideation (with the intended heart) and the manuscript, draft 1.*

**1. Concept.** The premise is familiar: a child waits at a harbour for a fisherman, and a light finds him in the fog. Close comparables are *The Storm Whale* (Benji Davies), *Hello Lighthouse* (Sophie Blackall) and *Harbor* (Donald Crews). The new part is the counting frame that breaks at nine, and that is the reason to publish it. The manuscript then spends that asset too quickly.

**2. Structure and page turns.** The counting game gets two real pages before it is folded up: three to five share a page, and six to nine share another. That halves the number of turns just as the rhythm should tighten. The 6→7 turn to fog is good. But the 10→11 and 11→12 turns carry no suspense. Ana raises the lamp, and on the next page "something blinks back". There is no beat of nothing, so the climax costs nothing. **Continuity error:** page 1 says Ana stands "at the end of the pier"; the fog then hides "the end of the pier", and she carries the lamp "all the way to the end". So where is she? A child listening will notice.

**3. Language.** The best line in the book is "A little light. A little boat. A little *blue* boat." It is musical and narrows like a lens. Most of the rest is serviceable stock. "The sun slips down behind the hill", "step by careful step" and "with the lamp to light the way" are clichés. "Fills" appears twice in two spreads. "Here comes a boat with a red sail" spends seven words on one number. The refrain "Not Grandpa's" never grows: it disappears at six to nine and returns only as its answer. A refrain should gather weight.

**4. The heart.** It is mostly acted, but it is softened. Mama was clearly added late: she appears out of nowhere on page 9 with a line of dialogue, and she has to be in the world from page 1. Grandpa's "I knew the way, but your light made it feel very close" reads as an adult explaining the brief. "Feel very close" is vague, and no fisherman talks like that.

**5. Text and picture.** "With a red sail", "with a green sail", "the sky turns purple" and "Mama walks right behind" all describe what the picture shows; cut them. Let the blue appear in the art before the words name it. The end page, with the lamp lit on its hook, should be wordless.

**6. The ending.** It answers the want, but "they all walk home together" is the most generic sentence in the book. It is inevitable without being surprising. The refrain is sitting there, ready to be turned around.

## The three changes that would most raise the book

**A. Give the count its full run, and break it on the number itself.**
> Red sail. *One!* Not Grandpa's. / Green sail. *Two!* Not Grandpa's. / *Three. Four. Five.* Not Grandpa's, not Grandpa's, not Grandpa's. / *Six, seven, eight … nine.* Not little. Not blue. / Ana waits for ten. / (turn) / But ten is not a boat. Ten is fog.

**B. Fix the geography, put Mama in from the start, and add a beat of failure.**
> p1: Ana sits by the old lamp at the start of the pier, Mama mends the nets, and Ana counts the boats home. … p11: Ana holds it high. Nothing. She holds it higher. (turn) p12: Out in the fog, something blinks back …

"Higher" is Ana's second, braver choice. That is where the heart gets earned.

**C. Turn the refrain around for the ending.**
> *Ten!* Ten boats home. And this one is Grandpa's. / (turn) / "I always know the way home," says Grandpa. "Tonight, I could see it." / He lifts Ana up, lamp and all. "And this one," he says, "is mine." / (end page: the lamp on its hook, lit, no words)

The child who counted everything is the last one counted.

**Score: 5/10** against award-quality picture books. With the three changes it could reach 7.5.
