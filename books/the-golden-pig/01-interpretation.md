# Stage 1: Interpretation

*Book 5 retells a Korean legend in Korean and English. It follows
[The Ugly Duckling](../the-ugly-duckling/01-interpretation.md): stage 1 is this
note, the reader's brief stands in for ideation, and a style study comes
before the words. It is an
[explicit-message](../../docs/process.md#explicit-message-books) book, for
ages 6–9. Issue: [#7](https://github.com/ArtColony/childrens_book/issues/7).*

## The brief (the ideation)

From the reader who asked for this book (2026-11-14):

> I want to create a children's book version of the korean story "The
> Legend of the Golden Pig". … I want it to be in the watercolor we've been
> using, and should have a distinctive Korean folk art style. It should
> highlight sunflowers throughout, and other traditional korean symbols. I
> want it written in both Korean and English, so both readers can
> understand. Violent content is fine if it is traditional, and an older
> audience is fine.

Their answers to follow-up questions:

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|

| How faithful | Faithful, for older readers (6–9) |
| Folk style | Minhwa (민화) in obangsaek (오방색), the five direction colours |
| Symbols | Sunflowers as hope; sipjangsaeng (십장생); magpie and tiger (까치호랑이); peony and lotus; dancheong (단청) and jogakbo (조각보) |
| Text | Korean in the sky band at the top, English in a quiet band at the bottom |

| How much story | **The whole novel, condensed**, to its ending on Mount Gaya |
| Is he the pig's son? | **Leave it a mystery** |
| Who defeats the pig | **The wife** |

## The source

The story is the Joseon-era novel *Choe Goun jeon* (최고운전, also 최문헌전 or
최충전). It is anonymous, existed before 1656, and survives in over thirty
copies. The golden-pig birth was told aloud before the novel was written:
유몽인 (1559–1623) calls Choe "the golden pig's child" in a poem. It is a
Korean form of the underground-monster tale (지하국대적퇴치설화). The full
beat list, the variants and the sources are in
[research.md](research.md). These are our main sources:

- 한국민족문화대백과사전, 「최고운전」: <https://encykorea.aks.ac.kr/Article/E0057190>
- 디지털군산문화대전, 「최고운전」: <https://gunsan.grandculture.net/gunsan/toc/GC05702130>
- Korean and English Wikipedia, *Choe Goun jeon*, for the fullest beat order

No English translation of the novel was found. The retelling is in our own
words, in both languages, and cites these sources on the last page.

Choe Chiwon was a real man. He was born in 857, went to Tang China at 12,
wrote the famous proclamation against the rebel Huang Chao, came home in 885,
and ended his days on Mount Gaya. The pig, the abandoned baby, the egg in
the box and the blue lion are legend. The last page says which is which.

## What we keep and what we change

We keep the novel's shape: **the pig is only the first third**, and the book
is the life of a boy everyone whispered about. We condense it into about
28 pages. These are the choices:

- **The mystery of his father is kept.** In most spoken tellings the boy is
  the pig's son, which implies an assault. The novel removes that: the wife
  was already pregnant. As the reader asked, **the book never settles it.**
  The baby is born six months after she comes home, with strange nails, and
  people whisper. Our text says only what the people said and what the
  father feared, never what happened in the cave. No version's sexual
  content appears in the book.
- **The wife defeats the pig.** Her husband's red thread finds the cave, but
  she gets the pig to name its weakness (deer hide on the back of its head),
  and she kills it with the deer-hide cord of her pouch. This follows Korean
  and English Wikipedia. The encyclopedia's version, in which the husband
  kills it, is noted in the research file.
- **The violence is kept and drawn plainly.** The vanished wives, the pig's
  death, a baby left by the sea, a threat of war, a poisoned meal and exile
  are all shown. There is no gore, but the pig dies on the page, as in the
  source.
- **These episodes are cut:** the Dragon King's son Yi Mok, the
  drought-island rain and the monk; the Tang scholars' verse contest; the
  exam and the Huang Chao proclamation; the river woman's talismans and the
  poisoned meal (magic that tests the charms, not him); and the
  talisman-throwing variant. **Kept:** the old woman's soy-soaked cotton,
  because it is a kindness and it later keeps him alive; the exile, because
  slander causes it and slander is the whisper again; and the blue lion,
  as one page of wonder on the way home (the editor would cut it; logged).
- **The marriage is Unyeong's choice.** A book that opens with stolen wives
  can't trade a daughter for a riddle. The boy comes into Minister Na's
  house as a servant after breaking a mirror by accident (Korean Wikipedia).
  Unyeong judges him by his work in the garden, and she chooses him.
- **The ending is the source's:** he and his wife walk into Mount Gaya,
  freely, at golden evening, which mirrors the first page, where wives were
  dragged into a mountain at night. He is never seen again: his hat hangs
  on a pine, and a path goes up into cloud. (Shoes by a stream, from the
  Hongryu valley legend, read as drowning to adults, so they're cut.)
- **Our one addition:** the father lives to see the end and says the
  lesson. In the novel he isn't mentioned after the boy leaves.

## The heart (explicit-message variant)

*Revised after the [editor's review](reviews/01-interpretation-writer.md).*

- **The question:** *if everyone says you came from something monstrous, does
  that decide who you become?*
- **For the child:** *what people whisper about you does not decide who you
  become; what you do does.*
- **For the adult:** the father is the story's real failure. A parent's fear
  of a story was wrong about the child, and his apology comes **before** the
  boy has done anything great, so the book does not say "worth is
  achievement".
- **The refrain**, the whisper, in fixed hearsay wording in both languages:
  *"The golden pig's child, they say."* / *"금돼지의 자식이래."* It is heard at
  his birth, from the officials at the shore, in the minister's house, and
  from jealous ministers at the Tang court. The scholars at the Tang court
  carried the rumour there.
- **The boy answers it:** *"My name is Chiwon."* (致遠, "reaching far"), when
  the minister asks who he is.
- **The boy's choices**, the source's own acts, which carry the heart:
  1. at three he refuses to come home to the parents who left him;
  2. he names the pavilion and comes home on his own terms;
  3. he names himself;
  4. he answers the box. **The box is the climax:** a sealed stone box, like
     the rock that opened at night, and "What is inside?" The egg that went in
     is not what is inside now. *"You put in an egg. What is inside now has a
     voice: a chick."* What he came from is not what he is now.
- **Said plainly, on the next-to-last page,** by the father, old, as his son
  walks into the mountain: *"When you were born, I listened to what people
  said. I should have watched what you did."* At the pavilion, mid-book, he
  only apologises: *"I was afraid of a story. Come home."*
- **The last line turns the refrain:** *"Once they said: the golden pig's
  child. Now they say: he became an immortal."* / *"금돼지의 자식이래… 이제는
  신선이 되었대."* What people say followed what he did. The mystery of his
  father is never settled.
- **What it is not:** "he was special *because* of the golden pig", or
  *because* of heaven. He **teaches himself his letters** from the birds'
  tracks in the sand (English Wikipedia: self-taught), and we draw it. The
  heavenly maidens nurse and shelter him; they don't make him clever.
- **The lesson gate:** fresh child and adult agents, given only the text, each
  say it back in one sentence. A Korean reader agent does the same from the
  Korean text alone.

## Symbols: what each one carries

| Symbol | Where | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **Sunflowers (해바라기)** | Every page the boy is on | Hope. They answer **kindness to him**, not his presence: they open where someone protects or loves him (the maidens, his mother, Unyeong, the old woman) and turn toward him then, and they stay closed or bowed where he is shunned. Key pages get a sunflower border. They are not a mark that he is special. |
| **Gold** | The pig, then nowhere, then the sunflowers | The one accent colour. Once the pig is dead the gold comes back only as sunflowers: the thing feared becomes the thing hoped. |
| **The red thread** | Pages 2–5 | The rescue. It returns as the thread of the jogakbo his mother sews for him. |
| **Deer (십장생)** | The cave and the mountains | Long life. Deer hide defeats the pig, and deer watch from the mountains at the end. |
| **Cranes, pine, clouds, peaks, waves, sun and moon (십장생)** | Landscapes throughout | The world as minhwa paints it: auspicious, patterned, alive. |
| **Lotus** | The seaside where he is left | The source's lotus and white birds tend the baby. Purity out of the mud. |
| **Magpie and tiger** | The minister's house, the Tang court | The magpie brings good news (the chick in the box). The tiger is the Emperor's threat, a comic minhwa tiger. |
| **Peony** | Minister Na's garden; the wedding | Wealth and honour, the life he earns. |
| **Dancheong** | Eaves of the magistrate's office and palaces | Bands under every roof, so the building says where we are. |
| **Jogakbo** | Wrapping cloths, the back endpapers, the text panel | The mother's patchwork: many scraps, one cloth. |

**Anachronisms, chosen and stated.** Sunflowers came from the Americas long
after Silla. Minhwa and jogakbo are Joseon. The book is painted the way a
Joseon folk painter might have imagined the old tale, with the reader's
sunflowers added to it, and the last page says so in both languages.

## World note

- **Setting:** Silla. The town of Munchang under steep minhwa mountains; the
  rock-crack cave; the seaside and the pavilion Woryeongdae; the capital;
  the sea crossing; the Tang court; a barren island; Mount Gaya.
- **Palette:** obangsaek on hanji. Blue and jade mountains and sea, red for
  the thread and dancheong, white clouds and cranes, ink black. **Gold is
  the one accent:** the pig, then the sunflowers.
- **Light across the book:** night for the pig (pages 1–6, moon and lamps),
  dawn for the birth, bright seaside days and moonlit nights, the
  full-colour courts, a grey island, and golden evening on Mount Gaya.
- **Cast:** the golden pig; the wife (his mother); Choe Chung (the
  magistrate, his father); the boy (baby, age 3, about 10, 12, a grown
  scholar); Minister Na and his daughter Unyeong (운영); the Tang Emperor;
  heavenly maidens and scholars; an old woman; a woman by the river.

## Overrides for ages 6–9

- **Length:** about 28 story pages, plus a cover and a last page about the
  sources. **About 30 English words a page (38 at most), about 850 in all**,
  and the Korean to match. This was measured at the second style pass, where
  a 35-word page filled the bands. The first plan's 50 words a page and
  1,000–1,400 in total didn't fit two languages on one page.
- **Type:** English in NewCenturySchlbk **19 pt** (21 pt took four or five
  lines and overflowed the band), Korean in NotoSansKR **20 pt**
  (`cb-textko`), and the title in NanumBrushScript (`cb-brushko`). The Korean
  band holds 3 lines and the English band 4.
- **Reviewer briefs:** `reviewer-child` is told the listener is 7–8, not 5½,
  and `reviewer-educator` is told the class is first or second grade. The
  parent brief allows traditional violence and asks only about how it is
  handled. A new `reviewer-korean` reads the Korean as a native parent would.
- **People:** this book can't leave its people out. The style study tests
  how to paint them before anything else.
