# Stage 1: Interpretation

*Book 4 retells a public-domain classic. The story already exists, so this
book spends its stages on the **art**. Stages 1 and 2 shrink to this
interpretation note and a retelling. A style-exploration step is added
before the hero sheet, and the artist panel's score is the main gate.*

## The source

Hans Christian Andersen, *Den grimme Ælling* (1843). The English text we
read is from **Hans Andersen's Fairy Tales, First Series**, edited by
J. H. Stickney (Ginn and Company, Boston; copyright 1886 and 1914),
[Project Gutenberg ebook #32571](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/32571).
Gutenberg marks it public domain in the USA. The translator isn't named:
Stickney's preface says the text was made by "a sentence-by-sentence
comparison of the four or five translations current". The retelling is our
own wording, and quotations from the 1886 text are marked.

The look is our own. It isn't modelled on any illustrated edition.

## Why this tale

We chose the tale by what the medium paints well:

| Tale | Mostly | People | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The Ugly Duckling** (Andersen) | birds, water, reeds and **four seasons** | one or two, and they can be left out | **chosen**: a year of landscapes, all birds |
| The North Wind and the Sun (Aesop) | weather | one traveller, the centre of every page | close second; but *Puff* is already a weather book with faces |
| The Tortoise and the Hare (Aesop) | a meadow race | none | too close to *Moss* |
| The Lion and the Mouse (Aesop) | jungle, a net | none | the same message as *Puff* |

## What Andersen's story says, and what we keep

The tale's arc: a grey chick, hatched last and largest, is mocked in the duck
yard, runs away, is scorned on the moor and in a cottage, and survives
a winter alone. In spring he finds he has grown into a swan.

Andersen ends with a line that bothers modern readers: *"To be born in a
duck's nest in a farmyard is of no consequence to a bird if it is hatched
from a swan's egg"* (1886 text). Read plainly, it says worth comes from
birth: he was always better than the ducks. We **leave that line out**.

Our reading, which the book will say plainly (this is an
[explicit-message](../../docs/process.md#explicit-message-books) book like
*Puff*):

- **For the child:** *You are not wrong for being different. You are still
  growing into who you are.*
- **Said in the book, on the last page:** the duckling was never ugly, only
  different and not yet grown. Wording is set at stage 2.
- **The second, quieter lesson (for the adult):** the duck yard was wrong,
  not the duckling. The book shows the meanness without excusing it, and
  shows one kindness: the swans welcome him *before* he sees himself.

## What changes in the retelling

- **Shorter.** About 15 story pages, one per season beat.
- **The hunt is cut.** Andersen's guns and the dead geese are too violent
  for ages 3–6. The moor is kept as loneliness, not danger.
- **The cottage, with the cat and the hen, is kept** for comedy: "Can you
  lay eggs?" "Can you purr?" The child laughs at the hen's narrow rules,
  which is the lesson from the other side.
- **The farmer who rescues him from the ice is cut** (it means drawing a
  person). The duckling survives the winter in the reeds, and the thaw
  frees him. It's a quieter, more painterly winter.
- **The ending:** the swans swim to him, bow and welcome him, and *then*
  he sees his reflection. Welcome comes first, recognition after.

## The art is the point

This book's job is to be **beautiful**: the seasons are the story, and
each spread is a landscape painting. The art direction is chosen in the
style exploration (stage 1b). Three looks are painted on one key scene: the
autumn evening when the swans fly south over the marsh and the small
grey bird watches from the reeds. Candidates:

1. **The house style** (`cb_kit`): watercolour wash and ink line, as in
   *Moss* and *Puff*. It is the baseline.
2. **Painted landscape:** paintkit's oil and gouache brushes (`pkbroad`
   skies, `pkdab` and `pkfan` reeds and trees, `pkwet` mist, `pktrowel`
   water), with no ink outlines, so form comes from colour and light.
3. **A colour print:** flat overlapping shapes in a limited palette of
   five or six inks on textured paper, with a slight misregistration. This
   is the look of a hand-pulled relief print. PostScript is a natural fit
   for flat shapes.

The artist reviewer and the advisor pick one, and the reader sees the
style sheet.
